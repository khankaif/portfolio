## The pain

Every custom piece starts as a CAD file. A manager gets the brief, hands it to a designer, QC checks the file, and it goes back for revisions until it's right. At month end, designers are paid by the points they've earned.

All of that lived in chat threads, shared-drive links and a spreadsheet. Nobody could see who was working on what, revisions overwrote each other, and payout day meant rebuilding a month of history by hand.

## Why it mattered

CAD is the first step of every made-to-order piece, so a slow or lost CAD delays the whole order. And when designers are paid by the piece, a payout dispute is a trust problem, not just a bookkeeping one.

## The bet

Model the work as a pipeline, not a to-do list. Every task moves through explicit stages, every revision is a new version under the same task, and payouts are calculated from the stage history, never typed in.

## Decisions

### Who is allowed to change a task's status?

Nobody, directly. Status is derived from stage transitions, and the stage pipeline is the only code that writes it. Before switching over, I checked the derived status against every real task already in the database, so the migration couldn't silently change anyone's history.

### How do you pay fairly for revisions?

Points are credited once, on first QC approval. Revision cycles don't change a task's value. A rate card, editable only by managers, sets prices and points, and money-related screens have their own permission scope.

### How does each person know what to do next?

One work queue, filtered by role. Designers see their tasks, QC sees what's waiting for review, and managers see everything. Per-stage clocks show where time actually goes.

### How do you lead interns without becoming the bottleneck?

I own the data model and the stage rules, and give the interns well-bounded pieces with clear edges. One intern built direct-to-S3 multipart uploads with retries, so large CAD files never pass through our server. I review every change before it merges.

## What I'd do differently

I'd have written the stage model first instead of starting from a status field. Moving to stage transitions later meant backfilling every existing task, work that a day of upfront modelling would have saved.
